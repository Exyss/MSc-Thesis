\chapter{Proof complexity} \label{chap:proof_complexity}

\section{Propositional proof systems}

Proof complexity is the branch of computational complexity and logic that studies the resources that are needed in order to \textit{certify} mathematical statements, rather than of the resources needed to decide them \cite{kraj_book, arora_barak}. At its core, proof complexity asks if there is a way of writing down proofs of propositional tautologies so that every tautology has short proofs. Such ways of proof writing can be viewed as sets of rules capable of manipulating propositional formulas, deriving new formulas from them. These sets of rules are referred to as \textit{propositional proof systems}. When a formula $\psi$ can be derived from a formula $\varphi$ through the rules of a proof system $Q$, we write $\varphi \stackrel{Q}{\vdash} \psi$ or simply $\varphi \vdash \psi$ when the context is clear. The easiest example of propositional proof system is \textit{Resolution}, based on two single rules:
\[\dfrac{C \lor x \quad D \lor \lnot{x}}{C \lor D} \quad \text{(resolution)} \qquad\qquad \dfrac{C}{C \lor B} \quad \text{(weakening)}\]

For a more detailed discussion on examples of proof systems, see \Cref{sec:proof_systems}. In their seminal paper, \textcite{cook_proof_compx} defined proof systems as surjective functions $Q : \Sigma^* \to \mathrm{TAUT}$, where the latter is the set of all tautologies, $\mathrm{TAUT} = \{\abk{\varphi} \mid \varphi \text{ a DNF tautology}\}$. The idea behind this definition is that of an algorithm that is able to determine which tautology is proven by a given proof. A modern and more descriptive definition can be achieved through the concept of verifier.

\begin{definition}
    A propositional proof system (p.p.s.) is a verifier $Q$ such that for all formulas $\varphi$ it holds that $\varphi \in \mathrm{TAUT}$ if and only if there is a proof $\pi \in \Sigma^*$ such that $Q(\varphi, \pi) = 1$ and $Q$ runs in time $O((\abs{\varphi} + \abs{\pi})^c)$ for some $c > 0$.
\end{definition}

This if and only if definition can be split in two implications called completeness and soundness. A proof system $Q$ is \textit{complete} if whenever a formula is a tautology it also has a $Q$-proof. Vice versa, $Q$ is \textit{sound} if whenever a formula has a $Q$-proof it also is a tautology. When $\pi$ is a $Q$-proof of $\varphi$, we often write $\pi : Q \vdash \varphi$.

When for each tautology there is a short proof, i.e. $\abs{\pi} = O(\abs{\varphi}^c)$ for some $c > 0$, we say that the proof system is \textit{polynomially bounded}. Proof systems were originally introduced by Cook and Reckhow as a possible way of attacking the $\mathsf{NP}$ vs. $\mathsf{coNP}$ problem. In fact, through the modern definition, finding a polynomially bounded p.p.s. is trivially equivalent to proving that $\mathsf{NP} = \mathsf{coNP}$ \cite{cook_proof_compx}.

\begin{proposition}
    There is a polynomially bounded p.p.s. if and only if $\mathsf{NP} = \mathsf{coNP}$ 
\end{proposition}

In their program \cite{cook_proof_compx}, Cook and Reckhow outlined a possible roadmap to proving that $\mathsf{NP} \neq \mathsf{coNP}$ through p.p.s. Their idea involved proving that strong proof systems require super-polynomial size proofs for some formulas, to then generalize the various results to any possible proof system, i.e. showing that every proof system has at least one formula that is hard to prove. However, researchers quickly realized that this task was becoming very hard even for weaker systems. In fact, after almost 50 years of research in the field, even weak systems have yet to be completely outlined (e.g. Resolution). 

The programme has also a second, entirely practical, motivation. Every complete algorithm that establishes the satisfiability of a propositional formula, referred to as a \textit{SAT-solver}, implicitly produces a proof of that formula in some proof system. For instance, the DPLL algorithm (Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland) \cite{davis_putnam, dpll} and CDCL solvers (Conflict-driven Clause Learning) \cite{cdcl_1, cdcl_2} produce different types of Resolution proofs, while Gr\"obner basis solvers produce algebraic proofs \cite{separation_pc_ns}. A lower bound on proof size in a system is therefore an unconditional lower bound on the running time of \emph{every} algorithm of the corresponding kind, regardless of how cleverly it is implemented. Proof complexity is, from this point of view, the theory of the limits of automated reasoning \cite{handbook_sat}.

\section{Common proof systems}
\label{sec:proof_systems}

\subsection{Resolution and Frege systems}

The Resolution proof system ($\mathrm{Res}$), introduced by \textcite{blake} and popularized by \textcite{robinson,davis_putnam}, is the most extensively studied proof system. It is extensively applied in areas such as automated theorem proving, SAT-solving and artificial intelligence. Like many weak proof systems, Resolution's proofs are actually \textit{refutations}, i.e. \curlyquotes{proofs} of unsatisfiability of a formula. This is a feature, not an issue: since $\varphi$ is a tautology if and only if $\lnot \varphi$ is unsatisfiable, any refutation of $\lnot \varphi$ is actually a proof of $\varphi$. In many works, the word refutation is often used as a synonym of proof. To help the reader, this won't be the case in this work.

Resolution refutations are based on repeated applications of two simple rules, starting from the axioms of the initial formula:
\[\dfrac{C \lor x \quad D \lor \lnot{x}}{C \lor D} \quad \text{(resolution)} \qquad\qquad \dfrac{C}{C \lor B} \quad \text{(weakening)}\]

Given a CNF formula $\varphi = C_1 \land \ldots \land C_m$, let $\mathrm{Ax}(\varphi) = \{C_1, \ldots, C_m\}$ be the set of axioms of $\varphi$. Then, a Resolution refutation of $\varphi$ is a sequence of clauses $\pi = (D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_t)$ such that for each $i \in [t]$ exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Axiom}: $D_i \in \mathrm{Ax}(\varphi)$;
    \item \textit{Resolution}: $D_i = \mathrm{Res}(D_j, D_k)$ with $j,k \in [i-1]$;
    \item \textit{Weakening}: $D_i = \mathrm{Weak}(D_j)$ with $j \in [i-1]$.
\end{itemize}

For the last clause $D_t$, it must hold that $D_t = \bot$, where $\bot$ represents the \textit{empty clause}, i.e. the clause that doesn't contain any literal.

The idea behind the Resolution proof system comes from the propagation of satisfiability underlying the resolution rule and the weakening rule.

\begin{proposition}
    Let $\alpha$ be an assignment. If $\alpha(C \lor x) = 1$ and $\alpha(D \lor \lnot x) = 1$ then $\alpha(C \lor D) = 1$. Similarly, if $\alpha(C) = 1$ then $\alpha(C \lor B) = 1$
\end{proposition}

One can prove by induction that if a clause $Z$ is derived through repeated applications of resolution and weakening starting from the axioms then $\alpha(\varphi) = 1$ implies $\alpha(Z) = 1$. When $Z = \bot$, we reach a contradiction since, by definition, the empty clause is unsatisfiable. Therefore, if we're able to derive $\bot$ starting from $\mathrm{Ax}(\varphi)$, we obtain a valid proof of the fact that $\varphi$ must be unsatisfiable. This proves the soundness of Resolution. Completeness can be proven similarly by induction on the number of variables.

For standard Resolution, it can be proven that the weakening rule is actually not necessary to achieve refutations. In particular, for each Resolution refutation $\pi$ there is an equivalent refutation $\pi'$ for the same formula $\varphi$ such that $\pi'$ never uses the weakening rule and such that $\pi'$ uses at most the same number of steps of $\pi$. This property will be useful to manipulate refutations by making assumptions on how and when the weakening steps are applied inside them. Notice that this holds only when deriving $\bot$ and not when deriving a generic clause.

For any fixed natural number $k \in \N$, we define an extension of the Resolution proof system called \textit{Resolution over $k$-DNFs}, denoted $\mathrm{Res}(k)$, first introduced by \textcite{resk}. This proof system works over sets of $k$-DNF formulas, expressed as a disjunction of conjunctions of at most $k$ literals. Its rules are inspired by those of Resolution, adapting them to manage $k$-DNFs:
\[\begin{array}{cr}
\dfrac{D_1 \lor \rbk{\bigvee_{j \in J} \ell_j} \quad D_2 \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j \in J} \lnot \ell_j}}{D_1 \lor D_2} & \quad \text{(cut)} \\\\

\dfrac{D_1 \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j \in J} \ell_j} \quad D_2 \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j' \in J'} \ell_{j'}}}{D_1 \lor D_2 \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j \in J \cup J'} \ell_j}} &\quad \text{($\land$-introduction)} \\\\

\dfrac{D_1}{D_1 \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j \in J} \ell_j}} \qquad \dfrac{D \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j \in J \cup J'} \ell_{j}}}{D_1 \lor \rbk{\bigwedge_{j \in J} \ell_j}} &\quad \text{(weakenings)}
\end{array}\]
where $D_1$ and $D_2$ are $k$-DNF formulas. Notice that the weakening removal doesn't work for this variant of Resolution.

\textit{Frege systems} are another classical type of propositional proof system, whose proofs are intended to infer tautologies. These proof systems are well known in the classical Logic literature and, furthermore, they work not only with CNF formulas, but also with general Boolean formulas \cite{kraj_book,galesi_toran}.

Formally, a Frege system is a propositional proof system built from a finite set of axiom schemes and a finite set of rules of inference that can be applied to sets of formulas. An \textit{axiom scheme} is a meta-formula with variables over formulas that can be substituted by any formula. For instance, both $x_1 \land x_2 \to x_2$ and $(x_1 \lor x_2) \land x_2 \to (x_1 \lor x_2)$ are formulas that can be obtained from the axiom schema $(P \land Q) \to P$, where $P$ and $Q$ are variables over $\mathrm{Fml}$. A \textit{rule scheme} is a typical rule of logical derivation. A prominent example of a schema rule is the \textit{Modus Ponens} (MP), defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{P \qquad P \to Q}{Q}
\end{equation}

Modus Ponens allows to cut implications from formulas of the form $P \to Q$ once the formula $P$ alone is derived. We can define different Frege systems introducing different axiom schemes. Nonetheless, \textcite{reckhow} proved that all Frege systems defined over the same connectives are polynomially equivalent to each other, meaning that the axiom scheme and rule scheme chosen is not relevant, up to some polynomial factor (see \Cref{sec:simulations} for an explanation of polynomial equivalence). Therefore, we don't actually care about which rules and axioms are used in our Frege system. This allows us to restrict our interest to a standardized Frege system, known in the literature as \textit{Hilbert-style Frege System}, denoted with $\mathsf{F}$ or $\mathrm{Frege}$. The rule scheme of this proof system allows only the use of Modus Ponens over the following standard axiom scheme $\mathrm{Ax}(\mathsf{F})$ of Hilbert-style systems for propositional logic:

\[\begin{array}{clr}
    (1) \quad & A \to B \to (A \land B) \\
    (2) \quad & (A \land B) \to A & (\land \text{ rules}) \\
    (3) \quad & (A \land B) \to B \\[1em]
    (4) \quad & A \to (A \lor B) \\
    (5) \quad & B \to (A \lor B) & (\lor \text{ rules}) \\
    (6) \quad & (A \to C) \to (B \to C) \to ((A \lor B) \to C) \\[1em]
    (7) \quad & (\bot \to A) \\
    (8) \quad & (A \to \bot) \to \neg A & (\neg \text{ rules}) \\
    (9) \quad & A \to (\neg A \to \bot) \\
    (10) \quad & (\neg A \to \bot) \to A \\[1em]
    (11) \quad & A \to A \\
    (12) \quad & A \to (B \to A) & (\to \text{ rules}) \\
    (13) \quad & (A \to (B \to C)) \to ((A \to B) \to (A \to C))
\end{array}\]

Given a formula $A$, an $\mathsf{F}$-proof of $A$ is sequence of formulas $\pi = (A_1, \ldots , A_t)$ such that each $i \in [t]$ exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Axiom}: $A_i \in \mathrm{Ax}(\mathsf{F})$;
    \item \textit{Modus Ponens}: $A_i = \mathrm{MP}(A_j, A_k)$ with $j,k \in [i-1]$.
\end{itemize}

For the last formula $A_t$, it must hold that $A = A_t$. The soundness of $\mathsf{F}$ comes immediately from the validity of the axiom scheme and the preservation of validity induced by Modus Ponens, something that assumed as an axiom in standard settings of classical Logic. Completeness follows similarly.

\textit{Extended Frege systems} \cite{cook_proof_compx,reckhow}, denoted with $\mathsf{EF}$, are Frege systems augmented with an \textit{extension rule} that allows the introduction of new variables that abbreviate more complex formulas.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Extension}: $A_i = q \leftrightarrow B$ where $q$ is a new variable that never appears in $A, A_1, \ldots, A_{i-1}, B$ and $B$ is any propositional formula.
\end{itemize}

Intuitively, this rule allows us to write shorter proofs by encoding partial statements as single variables and use it in the proof, instead of carrying over the initial statement across the whole proof. In some sense, Frege proofs can be viewed as proofs that reason about polynomial size Boolean
formulas, while Extended Frege ones reason about polynomial size Boolean circuits \cite{kraj_book,jukna}.

\subsection{Algebraic and geometric systems}

\textit{Algebraic proof systems} are based on algebraic polynomial rings. 
These systems can be used to witness the fact that a system of polynomial equations over the polynomial ring $\F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is unsolvable; equivalently, that the polynomials do not have a common root.

Consider a CNF formula $\varphi$ defined over $x_1, \ldots, x_n$. The formula can be encoded as a set of polynomials such that the formula is unsatisfiable if and only if such set of polynomials has no solution. We map each clause of $\varphi$ to an equation in the following way:
\begin{equation}
    \bigvee_{i \in I} x_i \lor \bigvee_{j \in J} \lnot x_j \mapsto \prod_{i \in I} x_i \prod_{j \in J} (1-x_j) = 0
\end{equation}

where $I$ and $J$ are, respectively, the set of positive and negative literals of the clause. We observe that truth values are \textit{inversely represented} in this context: the value $0$ represents truth, while $1$ represents falsity. This comes as a byproduct of the value 0 acting as a way of \curlyquotes{deleting monomials} under restrictions, while assigning the value 1 simply removes the literal from the monomial. Some textbooks and research articles fix this issue by mapping $x_i \mapsto 1- x_i$ and $\lnot x_i \mapsto x_i$ in order to preserve the same truth values \cite{kraj_book,alg_nphard}. In this work, we'll be using the flipped truth values. 

For each clause $C$, its corresponding polynomial is denoted with $P(C)$. The formula $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable if and only if the system $\Phi = \{P(C)\}_{C \in \varphi} \cup \{x_i^2 - x_i = 0\}_{i \in [n]}$ has no solutions. In particular, the latter set of equations is used to restrict all possible solutions to those that give Boolean values to $x_1, \ldots, x_n$. After this transformation is made, the question of whether a formula is unsatisfiable is reduced to the question of whether a set of polynomial equations is solvable or not. Algebraic tools can then be used in order to decide unsatisfiability of the original formulas.

Two particular algebraic proof systems are extensively studied in proof complexity: Nullstellensatz and Polynomial Calculus \cite{nullstellensatz,separation_pc_ns}. \textit{Nullstellensatz} (NS) is a static algebraic proof system, i.e. one that doesn't use rules of inference, based on Hilbert's Nullstellensatz, which states that given $m$ polynomials $p_1, \ldots, p_m \in \F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, where $\F$ is an algebraically closed field, the system $p_1(x) = p_2(x) = \ldots = p_m(x) = 0$ is unsolvable if and only if 1 lies in their generated ideal. This is equivalent to the existence of $m$ polynomials $g_1, \ldots , g_m$ such that $\sum_{j = 1}^m g_jp_j = 1$. For any CNF $\varphi$, an NS-proof of $\varphi$ is the set of polynomials $\pi = \{g_1, \ldots, g_m, h_1, \ldots, h_m\}$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j = 1}^m g_jp_j + \sum_{i = 1}^n h_i(x_i^2-x_i) = 1
\end{equation}
where $p_j = P(C_j)$ for each $j \in [m]$.

\textit{Polynomial Calculus} (PC) is a proof system extending Nullstellensatz, based on two inference rules:
\[\dfrac{p \qquad q}{\alpha p+ \beta q} \quad \text{(linear combination)} \qquad\qquad \dfrac{p}{xp} \quad \text{(multiplication)}\]
where $\alpha,\beta \in \F$ and $x \in \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$. A PC-proof of the CNF $\varphi$ is a sequence of polynomials $\pi = (f_1, \ldots, f_t)$ such that each $i \in [t]$ exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Axiom}: $f_i$ is the polynomial of some equation in $\Phi$
    \item \textit{Linear combination}: $f_i = \mathrm{Sum}(f_j, f_k)$ with $j,k \in [i-1]$
    \item \textit{Multiplication}: $f_i = \mathrm{Mul}(f_j)$ with $j \in [i-1]$
\end{itemize}

For the last polynomial $f_t$, it must hold that $f_t = 1$. The soundness of PC can be proven in way similar to Resolution: if we assume that the system of equations $\Phi$ has a solution, the equality with 0 can be propagated down to the last polynomial $f_t = 1$, raising a contradiction. Completeness follows from the completeness of Nullstellensatz.

\textit{Geometric proof systems}, instead, are based on linear programming and optimization routines \cite{chvatal, gomory,jukna,kraj_book}. All such proof systems are variants of the \textit{Cutting Planes} (CP) proof system, a refutational method derived from 0-1 integer programming. In CP, any CNF formula $\varphi$ is converted into an equivalent system of linear inequalities (similar to how algebraic systems convert them into equations) whose integer binary solutions correspond directly to the satisfying assignments of $\varphi$. Each clause of a formula $\varphi$ is mapped to an inequality in the following way:
\begin{equation}
    \bigvee_{i \in I} x_i \lor \bigvee_{j \in J} \lnot x_j \mapsto \sum_{i \in I} x_i + \sum_{j \in J} (1-x_j) \geq 1
\end{equation}

For each clause $C$, its corresponding inequality is denoted with $E(C)$. Interpreting truthness as 1 and falseness as 0, the formula $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable if and only if the system $\Phi  = \{E(C)\}_{C \in \varphi} \cup \{x_i \geq 0; x_i \leq 1\}_{i \in [n]}$ has no integer solution. The set of all feasible solutions for this system yield a polytope $P$. While any Boolean point in $P$ yields a satisfying assignment, an unsatisfiable formula $\varphi$ may still leave non-integer (fractional) points within $P$. CP eliminates these fractional solutions using the \textit{Chvátal-Gomory cut rule} \cite{chvatal, gomory}. In particular, CP is based on the two following rules:
\[\dfrac{\sum_{i \in [n]} \alpha_i x_i \geq \gamma \qquad \sum_{i \in [n]} \beta_{i} x_{i} \geq \delta}{\sum_{i \in [n]} (\alpha_i + \beta_i) x_i \geq \gamma + \delta} \quad \text{(linear combination)}\]
\[\dfrac{\sum_{i \in [n]}  \eta\alpha_i x_i \geq \gamma}{\sum_{i \in [n]} \alpha_ix_i \geq \floor{\frac{\gamma}{\eta}}} \quad \text{(cut)}\]

Formally, a CP-proof of the CNF $\varphi$ is a sequence of inequalities $\pi = (f_1, \ldots, f_t)$ such that each $i \in [t]$ exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Axiom}: $f_i \in \Phi$
    \item \textit{Linear combination}: $f_i = \mathrm{Sum}(f_j, f_k)$ with $j,k \in [i-1]$
    \item \textit{Cut}: $f_i = \mathrm{Cut}(f_j)$ with $j \in [i-1]$
\end{itemize}

For the last inequality $f_t$, it must hold that $f_t : 0 \geq 1$ to ensure a contradiction.

We observe that the definition of Resolution refutation allows rules to be applied more than once onto each clause of the proof. There may also be multiple copies of a single clause, each obtained by applying different rules. When each copy of a clause is used only once as premise in the whole proof, we say that the refutation is \textit{tree-like} (named after the fact that the graph becomes a tree). This restriction implies that if we want to use a clause multiple times then it must be derived again, usually starting from the same axioms. The last node of the refutation is referred to as \textit{root}, while the initial ones are referred to as \textit{leaves}.

This sub-system of Resolution is called \textit{tree-like Resolution}, denoted with $\mathrm{Res}^*$. This particular system is extensively studied due to its one-to-one correspondence to the DPLL algorithm. The same idea can also be applied to other dynamic systems, obtaining sub-systems like $\mathrm{Res}^*(k), \mathsf{F}^*$ and $\mathrm{PC}^*$.

\section{Comparing the strengths of different proof systems}

\subsection{Complexity measures for proof systems}
\label{sec:measures}

Complexity measures lie at the heart of proof complexity. These measures provide a rigorous language to measure if a minimal proof must be short or long, allowing us to evaluate how different systems perform on proving a common statement \cite{kraj_book,arora_barak,galesi_toran}. Different measures highlight different facets of a proof, often interlinking them. By analyzing these measures, we gain a deeper understanding of the inherent difficulty of logical reasoning, even from an algorithmic perspective.

Resolution is the quintessential proof system when it comes to complexity measures. Consider a Res-refutation $\pi$. For any clause $C$, let $\abs{C}$ denote the number of literals in $C$. Our main complexity measure of interest is the \textit{size} of $\pi$, written as $\abs{\pi}$, corresponding to the total number of literals inside it, i.e. $\abs{\pi} = \sum_{D_i \in \pi} \abs{D_i}$. For instance, the refutation in \Cref{fig:res_example_1} has size $18$. In a natural way, the size of the proof corresponds to the number of symbols used to describe the witness of the verifier-based definition of the proof system. For any formula $\varphi$ with $m$ clauses over $n$ variables, the Resolution size of $\varphi$, written as $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi)$, is the minimum size across all Res-refutations of $\varphi$ (equivalently, across all refutations of $\lnot \varphi$).
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi) = \min_{\pi : \mathrm{Res} \vdash \varphi} \abs{\pi}
\end{equation}

Finding the Resolution size of a formula tells us the exact minimum number of symbols in a witnessing string for the given formula. Proving the existence of at least one formula $\varphi$, with $m$ clauses over $n$ variables, whose Resolution size is super-polynomial with respect to $\abs{\varphi} = \Theta(n+m)$ is sufficient to conclude Resolution is not polynomially bounded. \textcite{haken,arora_barak} was the first to show that one such formula is the Pigeonhole Principle formula $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_{n}$, proving that it requires proofs of size at least $2^{\Omega(n)}$. This result not only implies that Resolution is not polynomially bounded, but it also implies Resolution is weak against a wide number of formulas due to this combinatorial principle being at the foundation of mathematics. 

A natural complexity measure related with size is the \textit{length} of a proof, which measures the total number of clauses inside $\pi$, i.e. the number of steps in the proof. The Resolution length of a formula $\varphi$, written as $\mathrm{length}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi)$, is the minimum length across all Res-refutations of $\varphi$. It is straightforward to prove that $\mathrm{length}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi) \leq \mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi) \leq n \cdot \mathrm{length}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi)$ holds for any $\varphi$. For this reason, the concepts of size and length are often treated as synonyms in the case of Resolution. In this work, we'll extensively use size as a synonym of proof length.

Another complexity measure inspired by Haken's proof is the \textit{width} of a proof $\pi$, defined as the maximum number of literals across all clauses of $\pi$, i.e. $\max_{D_i \in \pi} \abs{D_i}$. The Resolution width of $\varphi$, written as $\mathrm{width}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi)$, is defined as the minimum across all proofs. \textcite{bs_wigder} proved that Resolution size and width are related to one another through the trade-off.

\begin{theorem}
    $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi) = \exp \left (\Omega \left (\frac{(\mathrm{width}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi) - \mathrm{width}(\varphi))^2}{n}\right ) \right )$ where $\mathrm{width}(\varphi)$ is the maximum width of any axiom of $\varphi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Omitted. The reader is referred to \cite{bs_wigder}.
\end{proof}

We observe that in the exponent of the right hand side of the equation we have the difference between the Resolution width and the width of the axioms in $\varphi$. Because of this, for the case of families of formulas with large initial clauses, like the Pigeonhole Principle formula $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$, this method can prove only trivial size lower bounds. Moreover, it was proven by \cite{galesi_optim} that this tradeoff is optimal, meaning that no better general tradeoff can be achieved. 

All Resolution measures carry over to $\mathrm{Res}^*, \mathrm{Res}(k)$ and $\mathrm{CP}$, with a small exception: in $\mathrm{Res}(k)$ and $\mathrm{CP}$, there is no real concept of width, implying that the above trade-off theorem doesn't have an analogous in both of these worlds. 

Moving to other proof systems, size and length measures are inherited by Frege systems in a natural way, but the relation between $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Frege}}(\varphi)$ and $\mathrm{length}_{\mathrm{Frege}}(\varphi)$ is not so straightforward anymore since the formulas inside the proof are not restricted to being clauses. Nonetheless, \textcite{reckhow} proved that they are still polynomially related to each other.
\begin{theorem}
    $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Frege}}(\varphi) = O(\mathrm{length}_{\mathrm{Frege}}(\varphi) \cdot (\abs{\varphi} + \mathrm{length}_{\mathrm{Frege}}(\varphi))^2)$
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Omitted. The reader is referred to \cite{reckhow}.
\end{proof}

Other than this, no general trade-offs are known for Frege systems. In addition, there is no concept of width (again, formulas are not restricted to being clauses). Nonetheless, there is another complexity measure of our interest called \textit{depth}, that being the maximum nesting level of logical connectives across all formulas of the proof. For general Frege system, there are no known formulas that require super-polynomial size proofs \cite{php_easy}. However, when restricted to $\Theta(1)$ depth, some formulas are known. This sub-system of Frege is known as $\mathrm{AC}^0$-Frege. For this particular sub-system, exponential lower bounds for $\mathrm{PHP}^m_n$ are known \cite{hastad, php_lower, php_lower_2}.

Both in Nullstellensatz and Polynomial Calculus, size is defined differently. The size of a polynomial $\norm{p}$ is defined as the number of non-zero monomials obtained by rewriting it as a linear combination of monomials, i.e. after \curlyquotes{expanding it}. The size of a proof $\norm{\pi}_Q$, with $Q \in \{\mathrm{NS}, \mathsf{PC}\}$, is the sum of the sizes of all the polynomials inside it. The values of $\mathrm{size}_{Q}(\varphi)$, $\mathrm{deg}_{Q}(\varphi)$ and are defined accordingly. 

Regarding polynomials, we have another complexity measure: \textit{degree}. The degree of a polynomial $\deg(p)$ is the maximum degree of all monomials inside it. The degree of a proof is $\mathrm{deg}_Q(\pi)$ is the maximum degree across all polynomials inside it. 
In particular, for a NS-proof $\pi = \{g_1, \ldots, g_m, h_1, \ldots, h_n\}$ over the system $\{p_1, \ldots, p_m\} \cup \{x_i^2 - x_i\}_{i \in [n]}$ the size and degree of $\pi$ are defined by:

\begin{equation}
    \norm{\pi}_{\mathrm{NS}} = \sum_{j = 1}^m \norm{g_j} \norm{p_j} + \sum_{i = 1}^n 2\norm{h_i}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{deg}_\mathrm{NS}(\pi) = \max_{j \in [m], i \in [n]} (\deg(g_j) + \deg(p_j), 2 + \deg(h_i))
\end{equation}

For a PC-proof $\pi' = (f_1, \ldots, f_t)$, instead, the size of $\pi'$ is defined as $\norm{\pi'}_{\mathrm{PC}} = \sum_{i = 1}^t \norm{f_i}$ and the degree is $\max_{i \in [t]} (\deg(f_i))$. By definition, its easy to see that degree acts as an exponential size upper bound for Nullstellensatz proofs: when expanded out, a polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $n^{d}$ monomials each with at most $n$ variables. For Polynomial Calculus, instead, an analogue of the Resolution trade-off can be proven \cite{pc_tradeoff}, which was also proven to be optimal \cite{lauria_optim}.

\begin{theorem}
    $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{PC}}(\varphi) = \exp \left (\Omega \left (\frac{(\mathrm{deg}_{\mathrm{PC}}(\varphi) - \mathrm{deg}(\varphi))^2}{n}\right ) \right )$ where $\mathrm{width}(\varphi)$ is the maximum width of any axiom of $\varphi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Omitted. The reader is referred to \cite{pc_tradeoff}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Simulations between proof systems}
\label{sec:simulations}
To compare the relative power across different proof systems, we define the concept of polynomial simulation (or $p$-simulation) \cite{cook_proof_compx}. This notion adapts the concept of polynomial time reduction from decision problems to proof derivations.

\begin{definition}[$p$-simulation]
    Let $P$ and $Q$ be two proof systems. We say that $Q$ $p$-simulates $P$, written $P \leq_p Q$, when there is a polynomial time computable function $f : \Sigma^* \to \Sigma^*$ such that for all tautologies $\varphi$ if $\pi$ is a $P$-proof of $\varphi$ then $f(\pi)$ is a $Q$-proof of $\varphi$.
\end{definition}

Intuitively, this means that there is an algorithm that transforms $P$-proofs of size $s$ for the formula $\varphi$ into $Q$-proofs of size $O(s^c)$ for the same formula, with $c > 0$. It's easy to see that for any dynamic proof system $Q$ and its tree-like version $Q^*$ it holds that $Q^* \leq_p Q$ since any $Q^*$-proof is also a $Q$-proof. Furthermore, it also typically holds that $Q \not\leq_p Q^*$ since many formulas can be proven more efficiently when not restricted to tree-like proofs. If a proof system $Q$ $p$-simulates another proof system $P$ and the converse doesn't hold, we say that $Q$ is \textit{stronger} than $P$. 

A classic benchmark illustrating this gap is the Ordering Principle formula $\mathrm{OP}_n$, which asserts that every finite strict partial order on $n$ elements has a minimal element. While standard Resolution can easily refute $\mathrm{OP}_n$ in $O(n^3)$ steps by reusing intermediate sub-refutations \cite{op_upper}, tree-like Resolution requires derivations of size at least $2^{\Omega(n)}$ \cite{separation_res*_res}. This concludes that Resolution is strictly stronger than its tree-like variant.

When two systems $p$-simulate one another, they are said to be \textit{polynomially equivalent}. Equivalence between proof systems allows us in many situations to choose the best one to work with based on the context. For example, tree-like Polynomial Calculus is polynomially equivalent to Nullstellensatz \cite{separation_pc_ns}, meaning that the tree-like restriction is enough to lose all the power obtained by making the proofs dynamic instead of static.

If both $p$-simulations are impossible, the two systems are said to be \textit{incomparable}. Resolution is known to be incomparable with both Polynomial Calculus and Nullstellensatz, implying that changing the context from clauses to linear algebra neither gives or takes away power to the proof system. The first non $p$-simulation comes intuitively: Resolution lacks native linear algebra operations, making formulas that rely on this hard for Resolution and easy for Polynomial Calculus. A typical example of such difference is given by \textit{Tseitin formulas} on expander graphs \cite{urquhart,tseitin}.

The second $p$-simulation, instead, comes from the encoding of negative literals in the algebraic context. Since negative literals $\lnot x$ get mapped to $(1-x)$, if a formula contains a clause with $n$ negative literals $\bigvee_{i \in [n]} \lnot x_i$, the corresponding polynomial will be $\prod_{i \in [n]} (1-x_i)$. When expanded out, this polynomial is formed by $2^n$ monomials, making the size of any of its proofs blow-up to exponential \cite{gaps}.

To solve this representation issue, it suffices to add \textit{twin variables}, i.e. an additional set of $n$ variables $\overline{x_1}, \ldots, \overline{x_n}$ representing the negated literal. This new proof system is referred to as \textit{Polynomial Calculus with Resolution} \cite{pcr}. Here, each Boolean literal $x_i$ is mapped to the algebraic variable $x_i$, while the literal $\lnot x_i$ is mapped to $\overline{x_i}$. The rules of the proof system remain unchanged, with the addition of new axioms $1 - x_i - \overline{x_i}$ for all $i \in [n]$, used to force $x_i$ and $\overline{x_i}$ to occupy opposite values to achieve satisfiability. Since any PC-refutation is also a PCR-refutation, it immediately follows that $\mathrm{PC} \leq_p \mathrm{PCR}$. The simulation of Resolution, instead, requires more attention.

\begin{proposition}
    $\mathrm{Res} \leq_p \mathrm{PCR}$
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Fix a formula $\varphi$ and let $\pi$ be a Resolution refutation of $\varphi$. Without loss of generality, assume that $\pi$ never uses the weakening rule. We can construct a PCR-refutation $\pi'$ for $\varphi$ by simulating each resolution step in $O(n)$ applications of PCR rules.

    Let $m$ and $m'$ be two monomials and suppose that we have derived $m \cdot x_i$ and $m \cdot \overline{x_i}$ in PCR. By applying $\mathrm{deg}(m)$ and $\mathrm{deg}(m')$ multiplication steps, we can derive the monomials $m \cdot m' \cdot x_i$ and $m \cdot m' \cdot \overline{x_i}$. By summing them, we obtain the polynomial $m \cdot m' \cdot ( x_i + \overline{x_i})$. Similarly, we can multiply the axiom $1-x_i-\overline{x_i}$ by $m$ and $m'$ to get $m \cdot m' \cdot (1-x_i-\overline{x_i})$. Finally, by subtracting the two polynomials we obtain $m \cdot m'$ with a total of $O(n)$ steps.

    It's easy to see that the above procedure can be used to simulate Resolution steps. Starting from the axioms, we inductively map each clause $C$ of $\pi$ to a monomial $m_C$. For the axioms, the standard conversion suffices. Now, consider any inner clause $C = A \lor B$ derived through a resolution step over $A \lor x$ and $D \lor \lnot x$. Since these two clauses have already been mapped, respectively, to the monomials $m_{A \lor x} = m_C \cdot x$ and $m_{D \lor \lnot x} = m_C \lor \overline{x}$, the above procedure allows us to derive $m_C$ in $O(n)$ steps. By applying the procedure on the whole proof $\pi$, we obtain a PCR-proof $\pi'$ of size $O(n \abs{\pi})$. 
\end{proof}

\section{Game characterizations of Resolution measures}
\label{sec:resolution_games}

To simplify working with Resolution measures, we're often interested in characterizing them through \textit{games}. Besides being useful for proving width bounds, these characterizations depend only on the structure of the formula and can be defined independently of the concept of Resolution. This approach allows us to not only simplify proofs, but it also allows us to get insight not only on \textit{how} hard the formula is, but also \textit{why} it is hard. 

The first example that we're going to see is the so called \textit{Prover-Delayer game} \cite{pudlak_dpll, pudlak_games}. The game is played by two entities, a Prover and a Delayer, over a fixed CNF formula $\varphi$. The \textit{Prover} wants to prove that $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable and wants to do so as quickly as possible, while the \textit{Delayer} wants to slow down the Prover as much as possible.

\begin{definition}
    The Prover-Delayer game is a two-player game played as follows. On each round, the Prover queries a variable $x$ of $\varphi$ (which may or may not be a variable never queried before) and the Delayer has to answer with one of three options: \curlyquotes{it's true}, \curlyquotes{it's false} or \curlyquotes{you choose}. These options are represented by the values $1, 0$ and $*$. Whenever the Delayer answers with the third option, the Prover chooses an answer $b \in \{0,1\}$ and, if they are not yet able to prove that the formulas is unsatisfiable, the Delayer scores a \textit{point}. When the union of all values assigned by both players falsifies $\varphi$ or when the Duplicator gives an answer inconsistent with previous ones, the Prover wins the game. 
\end{definition}

The objective of the Delayer is to define a general strategy that allows them to score as many points as possible for any possible strategy of the Prover. We denote by $\mathrm{Del}(\varphi)$ the maximum number of points that Delayer can score while playing the game on $\varphi$ against an optimal Prover strategy. We notice that, since the formulas used in the game are unsatisfiable, the Prover can always win by querying each variable of $\varphi$, thus $\mathrm{Del}(\varphi) \leq \abs{\mathrm{Vars}(\varphi)}$ always holds. Any Delayer strategy corresponds to a lower bound on $\mathrm{Del}(\varphi)$, while any Prover strategy corresponds to an upper bound. In their strategies, both parties usually keep track of the game through some data structure, typically a partial assignment.

To show how the game works, we give an optimal Delayer strategy and an optimal Prover strategy for the Pigeonhole Principle formula, $\mathrm{PHP}^{m}_{n}$.

\begin{proposition}
    $\mathrm{Del}(\mathrm{PHP}^{m}_n) = n$ when $m > n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    We start by giving the lower bound, corresponding to an optimal strategy for the Delayer. On each round, the Delayer keeps track of the game through a partial assignment $\alpha$. When the Prover queries a variable $p_{i,j}$, the Delayer checks if $\alpha(p_{i,j}) \neq *$. If this holds, the Delayer answers with $\alpha(p_{i,j})$ in order to be consistent with their previous answer. Otherwise, the Delayer searches for a pigeon $i' \in [m] \setminus \{i\}$ such that $\alpha(p_{i,j}) = 1$. If such pigeon exists, the Delayer answers with $0$, otherwise they let the Prover decide the value $b \in \{0,1\}$, to then update $\alpha \gets \alpha \cup \{p_{i,j} \mapsto b\}$.

    It's easy to see that this Delayer strategy guarantees that no Hole Axiom $\lnot p_{i,j} \lor \lnot p_{i',j}$ is violated, thus the Prover can win only by violating a Pigeon Axiom $p_{i,1} \lor \ldots \lor p_{i,n}$. In order for this to happen, there must be a pigeon $i$ such that $\alpha(p_{i,j}) = 0$ for all holes $j \in [n]$. Each of these values must have been assigned either by the Prover or by the Delayer.

    Let $P = \{j_1, \ldots,j_t\}$ be the holes for which the Prover answered with $0$ and let $D = \{k_1, \ldots, k_s\}$ be those for which the Delayer answered with $0$, where $P \cup D = [n]$. All the holes of the first type imply that the Delayer scored $t$ points. For each hole $j \in D$ of the second type, instead, the Delayer answered with $0$ only because there is another pigeon $i^{(j)} \neq i$ such that $\alpha(p_{i^{j},j}) = 1$, but this is possible only if the Prover chose this value since the Delayer never actively assigns the value $1$. Therefore, these holes give the Delayer $s$ additional points, for a total of $t+s = n$ points. 

    For the upper bound, the following Prover strategy suffices. The Prover keeps track of the game through a partial assignment $\beta$ and a counter variable $z$. We denote with $\mathrm{Free}(\beta)$ the set of holes that are still unmapped by $\beta$. The Prover's strategy is based on sets of rounds, played as follows. On each set, the Prover subsequently queries the variables $\{p_{i,j}\}_{[n]\setminus \mathrm{Free}(\beta)}$ until the Delayer answers with 1. Whenever the Delayer answers with $b \in \{0,1\}$, the Prover updates $\beta$ with $\{p_{i,j} \mapsto b\}$. Whenever the Delayer lets the Prover decide, instead, the latter always chooses the value $1$ and updates $\beta$ with $\{p_{i,j} \mapsto 1\}$. If $\beta(p_{i,j}) =1$ holds after value is assigned (independently of which answer was given by the Delayer), the Prover increases $z$ by one and then starts a new set of rounds.

    In order to not violate any Pigeon Axiom, the Delayer has to eventually either answer with 1 or let the Prover decide, who also answers with 1. After $n$ queried variables are answered with 1 by one of the parties, $\beta$ describes a perfect matching between $n$ pigeons and $n$ holes. Thus, the next time one of them answers with 1 on a new variable, a Hole Axiom is guaranteed to be violated. Therefore, the Delayer can let the Prover choose for at most $n$ times, scoring at most $n$ points.
\end{proof}

The Prover-Delayer game can be used to prove lower bounds on the size in tree-like Resolution \cite{pudlak_dpll,arora_barak}. In particular, a lower bound on the number of points the Delayer scores in the game played on a formula $\varphi$ corresponds to a lower bound on the tree-like Resolution size of $\varphi$. 

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:prover_delayer}
    Let $\varphi$ be an unsatisfiable CNF formula. If $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}^*}(\varphi) \leq s$ then $\mathrm{Del}(\varphi) \leq \ceil{\log s}$. 
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\pi$ be a minimum size tree-like Resolution refutation of $\varphi$, where $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}^*}(\varphi) \leq s$. Without loss of generality, we assume that the weakening rule is never applied and that each variable of $\varphi$ is resolved only once on each path in $\pi$ from the root to an axiom. The idea is for the Prover to use $\pi$ as a template for the strategy. In particular, the strategy can be seen as a walk in the Resolution tree, going from the empty clause to some axiom. On each round, we keep track of the game state through a partial assignment $\beta$.

    Starting from the empty clause, at each round the Prover checks a clause $C$ in $\pi$. If $C$ was obtained by resolving two clauses $C_{0} := C \lor x_i$ and $C_1 := C \lor \lnot x_i$, the Prover queries $x_i$. If the Delayer answers with $b \in \{0,1\}$, the Prover updates $\beta$ with $\{x_i \mapsto b\}$ and moves to the clause $C_b$. When the Delayer lets the Prover answer, instead, they will do so with the value $b' \in \{0,1\}$ such that $\abs{\pi_{b'}} \leq \abs{\pi_{1-b'}}$, where $\pi_0$ and $\pi_1$ are the subtrees of $\pi$ with root $C_0$ and $C_1$, respectively, to then move to the clause $C_{b'}$.

    Assuming that the Delayer never contradicts themselves, the Prover will eventually reach an axiom $D$ of $\varphi$. When this happens, the partial assignment $\beta$ will falsify $D$ since we walked down the tree following the values opposite to those contained in $D$. Moreover, we notice that, each time the Delayer lets the Prover answer, at least half of the remaining subtree of $\pi$ gets discarded. Therefore, the Delayer can let the Prover answer for at most $\ceil{\log s}$ times before they win.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    $\mathrm{size}_{\mathrm{Res}^*}(\mathrm{PHP}^{m}_n) \geq 2^n$ when $m > n$.
\end{corollary}

We now move to another game characterization of Resolution measures, this time focusing on width \cite{atserias_dalmau}. The \textit{Spoiler-Duplicator game} is also played by two entities, the Spoiler and the Duplicator, who take roles similar to the Prover and the Delayer, respectively. This game is also played by answering queries on each round, but the setup differs. Both players construct a common partial assignment $\alpha$ for a fixed CNF formula $\varphi$. The goal of Spoiler is to falsify one of the axioms of $\varphi$ with the constructed assignment, while keeping a partial assignment small. The Duplicator, instead, tries to keep this from happening by deciding the values of the variables.

\begin{definition}
    The Spoiler-Duplicator game is a two-player game played as follows. Starting from the empty assignment $\alpha = \varnothing$, each round of the game is made of a query phase and a deletion phase. In the query phase, the Spoiler chooses a variable $x$ that is unassigned in $\alpha$ and queries its value to the Duplicator, who has to answer with either 0 or 1. After extending the assignment $\alpha$ with the answer of the Duplicator, the deletion phase is played. Here, the Spoiler chooses a subset of variables $y_1, \ldots, y_k$ that is assigned in $\alpha$ and \curlyquotes{forgets them} by removing them from $\alpha$. The Spoiler wins the game when $\alpha$ falsifies $\varphi$.
\end{definition}


We observe two main differences between this game and Prover-Delayer game. First, the Duplicator is not allowed to let the Prover answer. Second, when a variable is queried again after it has been forgotten by the Spoiler, the Duplicator is not forced to be consistent with the previous value. This last is a rule of the game that allows the Duplicator to keep fooling the Spoiler while he forgets values. In particular, we say that the Spoiler wins the \textit{$w$-game} if the number of variables assigned in $\alpha$ at the end of each round is always at most $w$ and, at some point of the game, some axiom in $\varphi$ is falsified by $\alpha$. If there is a strategy for Duplicator that prevents this from happening, they win the $w$-game.

A practical example of how the game can be played is delayed to \Cref{sec:rPHP}, where the game will actually be needed to prove some important results. For now, we focus on showing that the characterization holds. To do this conveniently, we introduce the concept of \textit{extensible family} of partial assignments \cite{atserias_dalmau}. A $w$-extensible family for $\varphi$ is a downwards closed set of partial assignments which do not falsify $\varphi$ and can be extended to any variable as long as they assign at most $w-1$ variables. 

\begin{definition}[$w$-extensible family]
    Let $\varphi$ be an unsatisfiable CNF formula and let $w \in \N$ be some parameter. A set of partial assignments $\mathcal{H}$ is a $w$-extensible family for $\varphi$ if all of the following properties hold:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
        \item $\mathcal{H} \neq \varnothing$
        \item if $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}$ then $\alpha$ doesn't falsify the axioms of $\varphi$
        \item if $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}$ and $\beta \subseteq \alpha$ then $\beta \in \mathcal{H}$
        \item if $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}$ and $\abs{\mathrm{Dom}(\alpha)} < w$ then forall $x \notin \mathrm{Dom}(\alpha)$ it holds that $\alpha \cup \{x \mapsto 0\} \in \mathcal{H}$ or $\alpha \cup \{x \mapsto 1\} \in \mathcal{H}$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    Let $\varphi$ be an unsatisfiable CNF formula and let $w_F > 0$ be the maximum width of the axioms of $\varphi$. Then, for any $w > w_F$ the following are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}[label={(\roman*)}, nosep]
        \item $\mathrm{width}_{\mathrm{Res}}(\varphi) \geq w$;
        \item There is a $w$-extensible family for $\varphi$;
        \item Duplicator wins the $(w-1)$-game on $\varphi$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii) Let $\mathcal{F}_{w-1}$ be the conjunction of all the clauses that can be derived from $\varphi$ by Resolution using only clauses of width at most $w-1$. We observe that $\mathcal{F}_{w-1}$ includes all the axioms of $F$ due to them having width smaller than $w$ by assumption, making $\mathcal{F}_{w-1}$ unsatisfiable, but it does not contain the empty clause $\bot$.

    Let $\mathcal{H}$ be the set of all partial assignments over $\varphi$ that have at most $w$ variables assigned and that do not falsify any clause of $\mathcal{F}_{w-1}$. We show that $\mathcal{H}$ is a $w$-extensible family for $\varphi$. By construction, the first three properties are already satisfied. By way of contradiction, suppose that there is a partial assignment $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}$ with $\abs{\mathrm{Dom}(\alpha)} < w$ such that $\alpha_0 := \alpha \cup \{x \mapsto 0\} \notin \mathcal{H}$ and $\alpha_1 := \alpha \cup \{x \mapsto 1\} \notin \mathcal{H}$ for some variable $x \notin \mathrm{Dom}(\alpha)$. Since the domains of $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$ contain at most $w$ variables, the only way for which they both don't lie in $\mathcal{H}$ is that they each falsify some clause $C = C' \lor x$ and $D = D' \lor \lnot x$ of $\mathcal{F}_{w-1}$, respectively. Moreover, $\alpha$ must falsify both $C'$ and $D'$, implying that $C' \lor D'$ is also falsified by $\alpha$. However, this latter clause can be derived by resolving $C$ and $D$, meaning that $C' \lor D' \in \mathcal{F}_{w-1}$, contradicting the fact that $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}$.

    (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii) Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a $w$-extensible family of partial assignments for $\varphi$. We give a simple strategy that allows the Duplicator to win the $(w-1)$-game. On each round, the Duplicator uses a partial assignment $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}$ being constructed during the game. By the properties of $\mathcal{H}$, this guarantees that $\alpha$ does not falsify $\varphi$. Starting from the empty assignment, when the Spoiler queries a variable $x \notin \mathrm{Dom}(\alpha)$, the Duplicator only needs to extend $\alpha$ to one of the two extension with $x$ that is guaranteed to be in $\mathcal{H}$.

    (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (i) We prove the contrapositive statement. A Spoiler strategy can be easily constructed by walking up the refutation and querying all the variables in the current clause and forgetting all the other variables. Since each clause has $w-1$ literals, the Spoiler wins the $(w-1)$-game once a leaf is reached.
\end{proof}
